II

God’s Man.

Who before the Lord shall stand? Who shall sit at God’s right hand?
He whose path of life is straight, Because to him the truth’s estate Is greater than a crownèd king’s: He who hates all whisperings Against his neighbor, speaking aye Only what his heart would say, And that in language loud, and clear Of all disguise, above all fear.
494
He to whom all guile is guile, All goodness good, all vileness vile; Whose promise made in very deed, Is sacred, let what will succeed: He who takes not usury,x And, though a world the bribe should be, In judgment never would consent To cast the helpless innocent.
Whose life is thus of God approved, He never—never shall be moved: Or onward only, so to run Duty’s whole circle, like the sun,— Through spheres more wide, for ends more vast, To rest in highest heaven at last.

x That is to say, excessive usury, under the latitudes and limitations of the idea conveyed by t term usury in its modern acceptation. Singularly enough, there was no one subject on which the ancient Jews were more entirely in the dark, than on this of usury—or the taking of interest upon money lent. Wholly misunderstanding the real nature of the thing, as a matter of commercial economy, or as it relates to capital, they wholly condemned it in their moral philosophy during their whole national existence, as a cruel and grasping exaction. Nevertheless, their instincts, from the first, were more than a match for their ethics. Sub umbra, and from a born bent in that particular direction, they had evidently tried their “prentice hand” upon it rather extensively even in the days of David—the supposed author of the Psalm of which the above is a paraphrase.

C.H.

Euroma, 1st. June, 1866.
